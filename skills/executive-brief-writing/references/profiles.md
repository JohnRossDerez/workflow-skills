# Executive audiences

Choose the reader purpose before the audience. Record who has authority, what they already know, which choice
or alignment problem they face, and what they should do after reading. Audience changes terminology,
compression, and emphasis; it never changes evidence thresholds, calculations, uncertainty, or material
limitations.

In the brief's reader-stakes card, also state the attention actually available, prior belief or investment,
what earns trust, likely resistance or accidental inference, and the one point to retain after closing the
brief. Use the card to choose what the main path must carry; do not mistake the executive's job title for a
complete model of this reader.

## Decision owner

Write for the executive accountable for choosing among alternatives. Lead with the recommended choice or
decision state, compare feasible options including no action, state decisive tradeoffs and constraints, and
identify conditions that would reverse the answer.

## Sponsor or resource owner

Write for an executive deciding whether to fund, prioritize, or unblock work. Connect the finding to business
consequences, resource needs, timing, dependencies, ownership, and the next commitment required. Separate
expected outcomes from scenarios and advocacy.

## Oversight or alignment group

Write for leaders who need a common understanding, risk posture, or policy position rather than an immediate
binary decision. State what is known, why it matters, where disagreement or uncertainty remains, and which
owner or event advances the issue.

For mixed audiences, write to the primary executive job and place specialist depth in an appendix or linked
technical document. Confidentiality, circulation, brand, and disclosure choices belong in the brief. A
preferred commercial position is an editorial constraint, not proof.
