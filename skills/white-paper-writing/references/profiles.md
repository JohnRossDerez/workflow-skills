# Internal technical audiences

Choose the reader purpose before the audience. Audience changes assumed knowledge, terminology, operational
detail, and emphasis; it never changes evidence thresholds, calculations, uncertainty, or material
limitations. Choose one primary audience and record it in the brief. Combine audiences only when the actual
internal readership spans them.

In the brief's reader-stakes card, also state the attention actually available, prior belief or investment,
what earns trust, likely resistance or accidental inference, authority or feasible action, and the one point
to retain after closing the paper. Use the card to decide which method and boundary details belong in the
main path; a technical job title alone does not establish what this reader knows.

## Builders

Write for engineers and analysts who may implement, operate, or extend the work. Make interfaces, data flow,
definitions, assumptions, algorithms, failure modes, dependencies, and operational constraints explicit.
Distinguish observed behavior, expected behavior, and unresolved implementation risk.

## Researchers

Write for readers assessing a method or empirical result. Make the question, competing explanations,
identification limits, method, evidence, robustness checks, and unresolved questions visible. Avoid treating
an exploratory analysis as confirmatory merely because it produced precise metrics.

## Assurance reviewers

Write for architecture, security, model-risk, governance, or technical-review readers who must challenge the
work. Surface controls, traceability, boundary conditions, sensitivity, failure modes, test evidence, and open
issues. Do not replace the technical mechanism with a compliance conclusion.

Confidentiality, circulation, commercial direction, and disclosure choices belong in the project brief. An
internal assertion or preferred conclusion is an editorial constraint, not proof. Company-specific styling
and logos belong in project assets rather than this skill.
